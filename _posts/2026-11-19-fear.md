How much place should fear hold in public discourse?<!--more--> And how should
that fear be calibrated to relative risk? Those are the tricky little puzzles
I've been thinking about recently.

## Road Safety

My 7-year-old gets regular school assemblies whenever there's a topic that
needs school-wide attention. The most recent one was on road safety, where they
were informed that "every 17 minutes, somebody dies or gets injured on the roads".

"What are you supposed to do about that?" I asked him — what were you taught
about road safety?

He said that the speed limit next to the school is 30mph, but that cars should
go slower than that because 30mph is dangerous near the school.

"But you don't drive a car — what can you do about that?"

All he could tell me was that cars should go slower, and that he should be
careful and look before crossing the road. I guess that's...fine?

I tried to talk to him about the number — I asked him if he thought that 'every
17 minutes' was often. I asked whether he thought that was across the whole
world or in this country. Whether it was mostly children or mostly adults. What
the number of people dying of heart disease was.

My wife, sensibly, stepped in and reminded me that he was seven.

It's sensible to teach children to be safe near roads, and to teach them how to
cross them safely. Beyond that, I'm not so sure there's much more to say or do.
Do we want children to be scared of the streets they live in? 

When I was fifteen, we had a woman come into our class to talk to us about
safe driving. She was incredibly intense, and berated us for half an hour or
more about how we'd never be good drivers because there was no such thing.
After she left, our usual teacher told us that her teenage son had died in a
car crash. 

Is it appropriate to expose children to all the potentially horrifying risks
that they're continually in contact with? A mother's grief is a powerful and
scary thing. At least a few children over a few different year groups at the
school died as teenagers in car crashes. Would more have died had that talk
not happened? We'll never know.

## Scams

Another assembly my son reported was one on online safety. He's been taught
about the value of rotating passwords, and not talking to strangers, and no
doubt a whole host of other things designed to keep him safe from a world he's
not part of.

He currently has exactly two passwords — one to the school Chromebook that he
sometimes uses, and one to his Times Tables Rock Stars account. Exactly how much
those passwords make him a target for cyber criminals remains to be seen.

I was listening to a Radio 4 show on consumer safety and there was a discussion
on card-reader fraud. Listeners were walked through the whole host of ways a
fraudster can trick you using handheld card readers, and were advised to always
demand to hold the card reader yourself for any transaction.

I think about various elderly people I've known who seem terrified of the
modern world, and I see where that comes from and why. Clearly elderly people
are the victims of scams of different types, and it's important to help them
avoid them.

But it's also important that the advice they're given is proportionate to the
risks involved (both the prevalence of the scam, and its severity). Whether
that's the case here, I don't know.

## Conclusion

These examples join a host of others that I've written about before: public
safety advice that highlights a risk and doesn't consider the cost of
mitigating that risk.

As far as I know, nobody has measured whether assemblies on road safety for young children reduce the rate at which children get hurt on the road. I'm pretty sure that nobody has measured whether they reduce the rate at which children play outside.

Does advising elderly people on the latest greatest scams keep them safer? Does
it reduce their mobility and likelihood of engaging with the world?

We feel confident giving advice, assuming that the advice has no cost and so
can only help. I don't think that's true.
